What we found

We tested three approaches against a shared set of 15 questions spanning clearly-answerable questions, semantically ambiguous ones, and five deliberately unanswerable "trick" questions with no matching policy. The rules-based keyword search answered instantly at zero token cost, but its lack of stemming caused it to miss a genuinely relevant policy when the question used a plural the policy text didn't ("product" vs. "products"), and a minimum-match threshold — tuned specifically to avoid confidently citing an irrelevant policy on the trick questions — also caused it to abstain on three answerable questions (warranty, whistleblower, and client-dinner expenses). The LLM without a vector index answered every clear and ambiguous question correctly by reading the entire policy database on every request, at a real efficiency cost of roughly 3,200 tokens and 3.4 seconds per question. The LLM with a vector index reduced average token use by roughly 8x (about 400 tokens) and was measurably faster, by feeding the model only the five most semantically relevant policies — this also let it correctly identify "Work From Abroad Policy" for a paraphrased question about working from a different country, something exact keyword matching could not do without shared vocabulary. All three methods correctly declined to answer any of the five deliberately unanswerable questions rather than fabricating a policy, so none exhibited outright hallucination in this test set; the two LLM methods did, however, differ in confidence calibration on one borderline question about expensing a client dinner, where the no-index method confidently cited a specific policy as directly applicable even though its text never mentions client dinners, while the indexed method more cautiously stated that no policy covered the specific scenario while still surfacing the same real, non-fabricated facts as context.

We recommend the LLM-with-vector-index approach for a production policy assistant. It matches the no-index method's accuracy on nearly every question while using roughly one-eighth of the tokens and slightly less time per query, and its retrieval step naturally scales to a much larger policy database — unlike the no-index method, which must re-read the entire database on every call, an approach that only gets slower and more expensive as more policies are added. Its main weakness is over-caution: on the client-dinner question, the Expense Approval Policy was among the five retrieved policies, yet the model declined to cite it because the policy doesn't explicitly mention client dinners, a failure mode that a prompt allowing closely related policies to be cited with a caveat could reduce. The rules-based method itself remains valuable as a zero-cost, zero-latency first pass that never fabricates content, but is unsuitable as a sole solution given its inability to handle paraphrased or semantically varied questions without shared vocabulary with the underlying policy text.
